## Why paleontologists don't clean fossils all the way in the field

When people picture a fossil dig, they often imagine scientists brushing the last specks of dust off a gleaming skeleton in the desert sun and lifting the clean bones into a truck.

In reality, **trying to remove all the rock from a fragile fossil outdoors is one of the fastest ways to ruin it**. Out in the field, wind blows grit into cracks, shadows hide tiny fractures, and removing the rock that cradles a heavy bone leaves the bone unsupported.

Instead, paleontologists treat **field work** and **laboratory work** as two separate stages:
- **In the field**, the goal is to locate the fossil, record its full context, and remove it safely **still cradled in a protective block of surrounding rock**.
- **In the lab**, where there are bright lights, microscopes, padded tables, and fine tools, the remaining rock is slowly cleaned away.

## How to build a plaster jacket

To move a fragile fossil and its block of rock across rough trails without cracking it, paleontologists wrap it in a **plaster jacket**—very much like the cast a doctor puts on a broken arm:

1. **Dig a trench around the fossil:** The crew digs a channel all the way around the specimen, leaving the fossil sitting on a sturdy pillar (*pedestal*) of rock.
2. **Add a protective barrier:** They cover any exposed bone with damp paper towels, tissue, or aluminum foil so the wet plaster will never stick directly to the fossil.
3. **Wrap with burlap and plaster:** Strips of coarse burlap fabric are dipped into wet plaster of Paris and laid crisscross over the top and sides of the rock block.
4. **Let it harden, then undercut and flip:** Once the top shell is rock-hard, the crew carefully chisels through the bottom of the rock pedestal, flips the block over, trims excess rock, and seals the bottom with plaster too.
5. **Label the jacket:** The field number, top/bottom orientation, and site code are written directly on the dried plaster with permanent marker.

![Five steps from carving a rock pedestal around a femur to paper separator, plaster-soaked burlap, undercutting and flipping, and a sealed jacket labelled DS-2026-04](images/lessons/fieldwork-03-plaster-jacket-steps.svg)

*The jacket cradles fossil and rock together for the trip to the lab.*
Small, sturdy fossils—such as single shark teeth or small shells—may simply be wrapped in tissue and placed in labeled sample bags or padded vials. Large jackets, however, can weigh hundreds or even thousands of pounds and may have to be carried out on stretchers, winched onto trucks, or lifted by helicopter.

## When the best choice is NOT to dig

Not every fossil that paleontologists find gets dug up and taken to a museum. Leaving a fossil in the ground is a normal, sensible decision in several situations:

- **Better studied in place:** A long dinosaur **trackway** with dozens of footprints across a rock ledge shows how animals walked together across an ancient mudflat. Cutting it into pieces would break up the very scene that makes it valuable. Instead, scientists map it, photograph it, make flexible rubber molds or 3D scans, and leave the real tracks in place.
- **Protected outdoor exhibits:** At Dinosaur National Monument, about 1,500 dinosaur bones are left partly exposed in a quarry wall. A building around that wall lets visitors and researchers see the bones in their original rock layer.
- **Not enough time or the wrong tools:** If a crew discovers a huge skeleton on the last two days of an expedition, rushing to dig it out would destroy it. Instead, they record its GPS coordinates, photograph it, cover it back up with cloth and dirt to shield it from rain and frost, and return next season with the time and gear required.

*Some finds teach most where they lie, so crews map and scan them in place.*
