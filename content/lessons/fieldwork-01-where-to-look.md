## Why paleontologists don't just dig random holes

If you handed a paleontologist a shovel in the middle of a flat, grassy soccer field and asked them to dig for a dinosaur, they would politely decline. Beneath the grass might be several feet of modern topsoil, and beneath that might be the wrong kind of rock altogether. Digging blindly has almost no chance of hitting a fossil.

Instead, finding fossils starts long before anyone picks up a trowel. It starts with **knowing where the right rocks are exposed**.

## Looking for sedimentary rocks

Almost all fossils are found in **sedimentary rocks**—rocks formed when layers of mud, sand, lime mud, or volcanic ash settled on top of one another and hardened into stone over time.

Common sedimentary rocks you will meet in paleontology include:
- **Mudstone and shale:** Formed from fine mud and clay on lake bottoms, quiet sea floors, or river floodplains. Great for preserving bones, shells, and delicate leaf impressions.
- **Sandstone:** Formed from sand in river channels, beaches, or dunes. Often holds sturdy bones, logs of petrified wood, and footprint trackways.
- **Limestone:** Formed mostly on warm, shallow sea floors from lime mud and shell fragments. Packed with fossil corals, brachiopods, clams, and marine fish.

By contrast, rocks that cooled directly from red-hot melted magma or lava (*igneous rocks* like granite or basalt) almost never contain fossils, because molten rock burns or melts organic remains on contact (with rare exceptions like tree molds in lava flows).

## Best places to search

Even if fossil-bearing sedimentary rock lies underground, paleontologists need places where **erosion** (or human digging) has peeled back the grass and soil so the rock layers can be seen directly:

1. **Badlands and dry canyons:** Sparse plants and seasonal rain carve steep gullies through stacked layers of mudstone and sandstone, exposing fresh fossils every year.
2. **Riverbanks and coastal cliffs:** Flowing rivers and ocean waves cut into banks and cliffs, slicing clean cross-sections through rock layers.
3. **Quarries and highway road cuts:** When workers blast or cut through hills to build roads or quarry limestone, they expose clean walls of layered rock that would otherwise stay hidden underground.

## Following the trail uphill

In the field, paleontologists spend hours—sometimes days—walking slowly along exposed slopes with their eyes fixed on the ground. This step is called **prospecting**.

Usually, the first thing a paleontologist spots is not a whole skull sticking out of a cliff. Instead, rain and frost break tiny corners off an eroding fossil and wash those fragments downhill. When a paleontologist spots a few scraps of fossil bone (often recognized by its porous, honeycomb-like inner texture or distinct surface color) lying loose on a slope, they:
1. **Do not dig into the loose dirt at the bottom of the hill.**
2. **Walk slowly uphill**, following the trail of weathered pieces.
3. **Stop right where the loose pieces end**—and look closely at the rock layer at eye level. Very often, the rest of the fossil is still sitting right inside that layer, waiting to be uncovered carefully.
