## Giving names to chapters in the rock record

Imagine trying to talk about a 20-story building if none of the floors had numbers or names. Every time you wanted to tell a coworker where a room was, you would have to describe all the bricks from the basement up.

To talk clearly about which rock layers a fossil comes from, geologists and paleontologists divided the global stack of rock layers into named chapters. This chart is called the **geologic column** or **geologic timescale**.

Importantly, **these names and boundaries came directly from patterns observed in the rocks and fossils**—long before anyone had laboratory machines to measure radioactive elements in minerals. When you hear a paleontologist say a fossil is "Devonian" or "Cretaceous," they are telling you *which chapter of the rock and fossil sequence* that specimen belongs to.

## The four major divisions (bottom to top)

Because rock layers build up from the bottom upward, geologists often draw the geologic column with the earliest layers at the bottom and the most recent layers at the top:

### 4. Cenozoic (Top / Most Recent Layers)
- **What the name means:** "Recent life"
- **Fossils commonly observed:** Abundant mammals (including horses, whales, mammoths, mastodons, giant ground sloths, and saber-toothed cats), birds, flowering plants, grasses, and modern-looking seashells.
- **Note:** Dinosaurs other than birds, and the coiled ammonite shells, are no longer present in these upper layers.

### 3. Mesozoic (Upper-Middle Layers)
- **What the name means:** "Middle life"
- **Smaller periods inside it (from bottom to top):** **Triassic**, **Jurassic**, and **Cretaceous**.
- **Fossils commonly observed:**
  - Land dinosaurs such as *Stegosaurus*, *Allosaurus*, *Triceratops*, and *Tyrannosaurus*, plus birds, which are a branch of the dinosaurs
  - Flying reptiles called **pterosaurs** (close relatives of dinosaurs, but not dinosaurs themselves)
  - Large marine reptiles, including dolphin-shaped **ichthyosaurs** and sea lizards called **mosasaurs**
  - Coiled marine shells called **ammonites**
  - Conifers, **cycads** (plants with stout trunks and stiff, palm-like leaves), and, higher in the Mesozoic, flowering plants

### 2. Paleozoic (Lower-Middle Layers)
- **What the name means:** "Ancient life"
- **Smaller periods inside it (from bottom to top):** Includes the **Cambrian**, **Ordovician**, **Silurian**, **Devonian**, **Carboniferous** (on many American charts, split into the Mississippian and the Pennsylvanian), and **Permian** periods.
- **Fossils commonly observed:**
  - Sea life, including **trilobites** (three-lobed shelled animals), **brachiopods** (lamp shells), corals, and **crinoids** (sea lilies)
  - Heavily armored fish such as ***Dunkleosteus*** in the Devonian
  - Coal-swamp plants: tree-sized club mosses (lycopods), horsetails, and ferns
  - Amphibians, and sail-backed ***Dimetrodon***. *Dimetrodon* is a **synapsid**, an early relative of mammals. It lived long before the dinosaurs, and it is not a reptile.

### 1. Precambrian (Bottom / Lowest Layers)
- **What the name means:** The thick foundation of rocks lying *below* the Cambrian period.
- **Fossils commonly observed:** Mostly microscopic fossils, including **stromatolites** (layered mounds built by microbes trapping sediment). In the uppermost Precambrian layers there are also soft-bodied sea creatures called **Ediacaran** animals. They had no hard shells or bones.

## A shared map for field scientists

Knowing these four big divisions—and a few famous periods like the **Devonian** (Paleozoic) or the **Jurassic** and **Cretaceous** (Mesozoic)—gives you the same map paleontologists use every day. If a museum label says a fish skull came from "Upper Devonian shale," you immediately know it came from the **Paleozoic** part of the rock column, below the dinosaur-bearing Mesozoic layers.
