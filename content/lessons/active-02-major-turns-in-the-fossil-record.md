## When many kinds of life disappear

Rock layers preserve a record of changing life. In an undisturbed stack, lower layers are older and higher layers are younger.

Sometimes scientists find a large change: many kinds of fossils occur in older rocks but stop appearing in younger rocks of the same region. When a similar loss occurs at about the same time around the world, it can reveal a **mass extinction**—many species dying out in a short time compared with most geologic change.

A missing fossil at one site is not enough. The animal might have moved away, its habitat might have changed, or its remains might not have been preserved. Scientists compare evidence from many places.

## One example: the end of the Cretaceous

About **66 million years ago**, a mass extinction marked the end of the Cretaceous Period. It also marks the boundary between the **Mesozoic** and **Cenozoic**, the larger chapters you met in Unit 5. Charts often shorten this boundary's name to **K–Pg**. You do not need to memorize the letters to understand the pattern.

Read the diagram from the older layers at the bottom to the younger layers at the top.

### 1. Below the boundary: life before the extinction

Fossils record dinosaurs such as Triceratops on land, and ammonites and mosasaurs in the seas. **Mammals and birds already existed too.** They did not first appear after the extinction.

The diagram combines examples from different habitats. You would not expect to find every pictured animal together in a single rock layer.

### 2. At the boundary: clues to a major disruption

At many sites, a thin clay layer marks the boundary. Researchers find unusual amounts of **iridium**, a metal more common in meteorites than in Earth's crust. Other clues include quartz damaged by extreme pressure and tiny droplets of melted rock that cooled into glass.

These clues support a giant **asteroid impact**. The impact and its effects caused severe environmental changes. Large volcanic eruptions were also occurring, and scientists study how they affected conditions around this time.

Keep two questions separate: **The fossils show what changed in life. The rock and mineral clues help explain why.**

![Photograph of the end-Cretaceous boundary exposure in Colorado, with a thin dark layer between thicker rock beds](images/lessons/photo-kpg-boundary-colorado.jpg)

*A real boundary exposure in Colorado. The diagram simplifies the pattern to make it easier to read; an actual outcrop has uneven layers and weathered surfaces. Photo: [Jeffrey Beall](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cretaceous%E2%80%93Paleogene_boundary_in_Colorado_1.JPG), [CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/), via Wikimedia Commons.*

### 3. Above the boundary: survivors and a changing world

Dinosaurs other than birds, ammonites, and mosasaurs did not survive this extinction. But **life did not start over from nothing**. Some mammals, birds, turtles, crocodile relatives, fishes, and plants survived.

That does not mean every species in those groups survived. Many suffered losses too.

Over time, the descendants of survivors spread into different ways of life. Mammals became more varied, and some grew much larger. These changes took many generations; large new mammals did not appear overnight.

**Check the diagram:** Find a group shown both below and above the boundary. Then find a group that stops at the boundary. What is the difference?

## Other mass extinctions left different clues

The end-Cretaceous event was not the only mass extinction.

| Event | A change in life | Main idea to remember |
|---|---|---|
| Late Devonian | Major losses among reef communities and armored fishes, including the group containing Dunkleosteus | The losses occurred through more than one episode |
| End of the Permian | The largest known mass extinction; trilobites disappeared, along with many other species | It affected life on land and in the seas |

Scientists investigate volcanic activity, climate change, changing sea levels, and changes in ocean conditions when studying these events. They do not assume that the asteroid explanation fits every mass extinction.

## Read the evidence in three steps

1. **Find the pattern:** Which fossils continue, and which stop appearing?
2. **Compare places:** Is the change local, or does it occur widely at about the same time?
3. **Investigate the cause:** What other clues in the rocks could explain the loss?

A mass extinction is a widespread loss of life. The layers above it show both the survivors and the long process of a changing world.
