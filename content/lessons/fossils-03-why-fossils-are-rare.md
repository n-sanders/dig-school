## What usually happens after an organism dies

Think about a walk through the woods or across an open field today. Hundreds of birds, squirrels, insects, deer, and trees live and die there every year. Yet the ground is not stacked deep with skeletons. Where does everything go?

In almost every case, nature recycles remains quickly:
- **Scavengers** eat the meat, scatter the bones, and crunch smaller skeletons.
- **Bacteria, fungi, and insects** break down soft tissues, leaves, and wood into soil nutrients.
- **Sun, rain, frost, and flowing water** crack, dissolve, and wear away exposed shells and bones until nothing recognizable is left.

Because these everyday processes are so thorough, **most living things never become fossils**.

## What tilts the odds toward preservation?

Whether an organism leaves a fossil depends heavily on **how it is built** and **where it lives**:

### 1. Hard parts vs. soft parts
- **Hard parts**—such as tooth enamel, clam and snail shells, coral skeletons, vertebrate bones, and woody tree trunks—are already rich in minerals or tough fibers. They hold their shape long enough for sediment to bury them. That is why isolated teeth and shells are the most common body fossils on Earth.
- **Soft-bodied organisms**—such as jellyfish, earthworms, slugs, and soft mosses—have no bones or shells at all. Unless they are buried almost instantly in extraordinary mud or resin, they decay without leaving a body fossil (though worms often leave plenty of **trace fossils** in the form of burrows!).

### 2. Burial environments vs. eroding environments
- **Places where sediment builds up**—shallow sea floors, river deltas, lake bottoms, and muddy floodplains—regularly blanket shells, bones, and leaves in protective mud or sand.
- **Places where rock is wearing away**—high mountaintops, steep rocky ridges, or dry upland forests—rarely accumulate thick blankets of fresh sediment, so remains lying there almost always break down before burial.

## Why every fossil is valuable physical evidence

Because so many things have to go right for a fossil to form—and survive underground without being crushed or eroded away before someone finds it—the fossil record contains only a **small sample of past life**.

That rarity is the exact reason paleontologists treat every well-preserved specimen with such care. When a fossil *does* survive, it is not a rumor or a guess:
- A fossil shell, tooth, leaf, or footprint is **direct physical evidence** of an organism that once lived.
- It can be **held in your hand, measured with calipers, examined under a microscope, and compared side by side** with other fossils and with living plants and animals.
