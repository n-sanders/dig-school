## Meeting a predator from ancient seas

Before we look at dinosaurs on land or mammoths in the Ice Age, let's travel lower in the geologic column to the **Devonian Period** of the **Paleozoic**—a chapter of the rock record so rich in marine fish fossils that paleontologists often call it the "Age of Fishes."

In the dark gray shales around Cleveland, Ohio, paleontologists uncover thick, curved slabs of dark fossil bone that fit together into a massive skull: ***Dunkleosteus***. Fossils of this kind of fish have also been found in places such as Morocco and Poland.

*Dunkleosteus* belonged to an extinct group of armored fishes called **placoderms** ("plate-skinned" fish). The largest species, *Dunkleosteus terrelli*, was one of the top ocean predators of its time. A typical adult was about 11 feet (3.4 meters) long, and the biggest known individuals reached about 13 to 14 feet (around 4.1 meters). Older pictures sometimes stretched it past 20 feet. Measurements of the head armor point to the shorter length.

## What the fossils directly show

When you stand in front of a prepared *Dunkleosteus* fossil, three observable features jump right out:

### 1. Heavy head and shoulder armor
Unlike modern bony fish covered in thin, flexible scales, the head and front of the body were encased in interlocking **plates of dense bone**. The thickest plate, at the back of the skull, reaches about two inches (5 cm). Most of the other plates are thinner, about a third of an inch to an inch.
- A joint between the skull armor and the shoulder armor let the fish raise its head and drop its lower jaw at the same time, so the mouth could open wide.

### 2. Self-sharpening jaw blades instead of teeth
Look closely at the mouth of a *Dunkleosteus* skull and you will notice something strange: **there are no separate teeth**.
- Instead, the jaw bones themselves formed thick, sharp-edged **bony blades** with fang-like points at the front.
- Every time the upper and lower jaw plates snapped shut, their beveled edges slid tightly past one another like a pair of heavy garden shears—sharpening themselves with every bite!
- The jaw joint and the scars where muscles attached show a cutting bite that could damage shell and bone. Much of the jaw was cartilage, so the exact force is still being worked out. The sharp blades and the bite marks are the clear evidence.

### 3. Why the back half is usually missing
In museums, you almost always see only the front armor shield of *Dunkleosteus* on display. Why?
- Remember from Unit 2 that **hard parts fossilize far more often than soft parts**.
- Only the front third of *Dunkleosteus* was covered in heavy bony armor. The back of its body and tail were supported mostly by **cartilage** (like a shark's skeleton), which rarely survives decay. Paleontologists reconstruct the shape of its body and tail by studying smaller, closely related placoderms whose full body outlines were preserved in fine shale, along with rare cartilage impressions.

## From fossil armor to a living animal

How can scientists picture the whole fish when so much of its body is missing? They build a **life reconstruction**: a picture that combines preserved evidence with predictions about the missing parts.

Start at the head and work toward the tail:

- **Direct evidence:** Preserved skulls and armor constrain the shape of the head and front of the body.
- **Evidence-based predictions:** More complete relatives help scientists estimate missing anatomy. Measurements and comparisons with living fishes help test proposed body proportions and swimming shapes. This reconstruction proposes a deep, sturdy body.
- **Greater uncertainty:** The exact outline of the tail and fins, and the animal's colors and markings, are less certain. The picture's dark back and pale belly are an interpretation, not colors demonstrated by this fossil.

This is careful, evidence-based guesswork. Different reconstructions can fit the surviving clues, and researchers can compare their predictions. A new fossil or a better measurement may change the picture.

## Evidence of diet and ancient ocean life

How do paleontologists know what *Dunkleosteus* ate? The fossils preserve direct clues:
- **Bite marks:** Armor plates of other placoderms—and even other *Dunkleosteus* individuals—have been found with deep, round puncture gouges that match the exact spacing of *Dunkleosteus* jaw tips.
- **Fossilized meal remains:** Lumps of partly digested fish bones and shark remains have been found with *Dunkleosteus* fossils in the shale. The blades may have been able to crack coiled shells too. The meals actually found beside the fish are mostly other fish.

By looking closely at its armor, jaw mechanics, and surrounding marine shale, *Dunkleosteus* gives us a vivid window into how large predators were built in ancient Paleozoic oceans.
