## Starting with things you can picture

Picture three objects lying on a worktable:
1. A ribbed **seashell** encased in gray limestone.
2. A **three-toed footprint** pressed into a slab of hardened red mud.
3. A heavy chunk of **petrified wood** whose growth rings and bark texture have turned into solid stone.

Even though one comes from a sea creature, one is a footprint with no bone at all, and one comes from a tree, all three are **fossils**.

A **fossil** is **any preserved evidence of past life**. It does not have to be a dinosaur bone. It can come from a clam, a fern, a fish, a mammoth, a beetle, or a microscopic water plant—and it can be either part of the organism itself or a mark the organism left behind while it was alive.

## Two main categories: Body fossils and trace fossils

Paleontologists group fossils into two broad kinds of evidence:

### 1. Body fossils
**Body fossils** are preserved parts of an organism's body (or mineral replacements that keep the body part's shape). Common examples include:
- **Bones and teeth** from vertebrates such as fish, reptiles, birds, and mammals
- **Shells and exoskeletons** from clams, snails, corals, and trilobites
- **Wood, seeds, cones, and leaf impressions** from ancient plants

A body fossil shows you **how the organism was built**—its size, the shape of its teeth, the structure of its shell, or the veins of its leaves.

### 2. Trace fossils
**Trace fossils** are preserved signs of an organism's **activity** rather than parts of its body. Even when no bone or shell is anywhere nearby, trace fossils show that a living creature was there and doing something:
- **Footprints and trackways** pressed into damp mud, sand, or wet volcanic ash
- **Burrows, tubes, and resting marks** dug into soft sea-floor or riverbank sediment by worms, crabs, or small land animals
- **Bite marks and gnaw marks** scratched into bones or bored through shells
- **Coprolites** (fossilized droppings), which often still contain tiny bits of bone, fish scales, pollen, or plant fibers from the animal's last meals

## Why both kinds of fossils matter

Notice how the two categories answer different questions:

| Question | Best answered by... | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *How many teeth did this animal have, and what shape were they?* | **Body fossil** | A fossilized skull and jawbone |
| *Did this animal walk on two legs or four, and were its feet side by side or placed in a narrow line?* | **Trace fossil** | A fossil trackway preserved in hardened mudstone |
| *What specific prey or plant did this animal swallow right before it left a trace?* | **Trace fossil** (containing tiny body fossils!) | A coprolite packed with crushed snail shells or conifer needles |

A bone shows an animal at the end of its life. A footprint or burrow captures a moment when the animal was **alive and moving**. Having both body fossils and trace fossils gives paleontologists a much richer picture of ancient life than either kind could provide on its own.
