Unit 1: Digging Up the Past
Approach

Introduce the shared idea of carefully uncovering physical evidence from long ago, then clearly distinguish archaeology (human-made things) from paleontology (evidence of ancient life). Emphasize the common methods and the importance of context so the rest of the course has a solid foundation. Keep the tone curious and practical rather than technical. Use simple, concrete comparisons a student can picture.
Key concepts

“Digging up the past” means slowly and carefully uncovering things that were buried or left behind long ago, then studying them to learn about earlier times. It is deliberate detective work, not a treasure hunt.
Two main sciences do this work:
Archaeology studies human cultures by examining objects people made or used (tools, pottery, buildings, clothing, and similar items).
Paleontology studies ancient life by examining fossils (remains or traces of plants, animals, and other organisms).

The two fields ask different questions and look at different kinds of evidence, but they share important methods:
Dig slowly and carefully so nothing is damaged or missed.
Pay close attention to layers. In most places, deeper layers were laid down before the layers above them, so they are usually older.
Record the exact spot where each item is found, along with what else was nearby. This surrounding information is called context.
Take notes, measurements, photographs, and drawings so the evidence can be studied later even after it has been removed.

A single object or bone by itself usually tells only a small part of the story. The layers, the other items found with it, and the precise location supply the rest of the information that makes the find meaningful.
Scientists dig to answer specific questions: How did these people or organisms live? What was their environment like? What changed over time? Careful records let them (and other scientists) keep answering those questions long after the dig is finished.
Not every interesting buried thing is studied the same way. A stone tool and a dinosaur bone both come out of the ground, but they are investigated by different specialists using overlapping but not identical skills.

Tone guidance for lessons

Start with the concrete activity of careful digging and recording rather than with definitions.
Use side-by-side examples (a pottery shard versus a fossil shell, or a buried house foundation versus a buried skeleton) to make the archaeology/paleontology distinction clear without implying one is more important.
Stress context and careful notes as the habits that turn a hole in the ground into useful evidence.
Keep language observational and practical (“paleontologists and archaeologists both…”) so the unit feels like an introduction to real work rather than a list of vocabulary.
End by noting that the rest of the course will focus on the paleontology side—fossils and ancient life—while still using the careful methods introduced here.
