Unit 2: What Is a Fossil?
Approach

Define a fossil broadly and concretely, then separate the main kinds of evidence students will meet later. Explain fossil formation with a few clear, simplified pathways rather than a single rigid process. Stress that fossils are uncommon so students understand why each well-preserved example is valuable. Keep the focus on what can be observed and how the evidence is preserved.
Key concepts

A fossil is any preserved evidence of past life. It can be part of an organism itself or a sign that an organism was once there.
There are two main categories:
Body fossils are actual parts of organisms (or their mineral replacements), such as bones, teeth, shells, wood, or leaves.
Trace fossils are signs of activity, such as footprints, burrows, bite marks, or coprolites (fossilized droppings). They show behavior even when the body itself is missing.

Fossils form in several different ways. Common simplified pathways include:
An organism or its trace is quickly buried by sediment (mud, sand, or volcanic ash), which protects it from being eaten or completely decayed.
Over time, minerals carried by water can fill in or replace the original material, turning it into rock while keeping the shape.
Sometimes the original material dissolves away and leaves a mold; later minerals fill that mold and create a cast.
In special cases an organism can be preserved in amber (hardened tree resin), ice, or tar with relatively little change.

Most living things never become fossils. They are eaten, decay completely, or are destroyed by weather and erosion before they can be buried and preserved. Because of this, the fossil record contains only a small sample of past life.
The fossils that do form give direct physical evidence of organisms that are no longer alive and of the environments in which they lived. A shell, a footprint, or a piece of petrified wood is something that can be held, measured, and compared.

Tone guidance for lessons

Begin with familiar examples (a seashell, a footprint in mud, a piece of petrified wood) before introducing the word “fossil.”
Use simple before-and-after sequences or side-by-side comparisons to show body fossils versus trace fossils.
Present the formation pathways as “one way this can happen” rather than as a single required sequence, so students see that preservation varies.
Mention rarity matter-of-factly (“only a few organisms leave fossils”) without dramatic language.
Keep descriptions observational and concrete so the unit stays focused on evidence students can picture, preparing them for later units on how those fossils are found and studied.
